# RPM primary-source notes (2026-09-12)

Scope: bounded extraction from Tony Robbins/RRI’s public RPM PDFs. These notes report what the sources show; they do not assess efficacy or prescribe product behavior.

Sources:

- [RPM Planner FAQs (RRI, 2019/2020)](https://go.tonyrobbins.com/hubfs/PDF/WP-Files/RPM-Planner-FAQs-min.pdf?hsLang=en) (FAQ PDF, cited by page number)
- [RPM Pathways to Power — Weekly Plan: Diego](https://go.tonyrobbins.com/hubfs/PDF/WP-Files/RPM-Pathways-to-Power-Weekly-Plan-Diego-min.pdf?hsLang=en) (weekly example, cited by page number)
- [RPM Pathways to Power — Categories: Amelia](https://go.tonyrobbins.com/hubfs/PDF/WP-Files/RPM-Pathways-to-Power-Categories-Amelia-min.pdf?hsLang=en) (categories example, cited by page number)

## Concrete findings

1. RPM is presented as a life-management system, not merely time management: decide what is wanted for life before deciding how to spend time. Its three-part framing is Result (what), Purpose (why), and a Massive Action Plan/MAP (specific steps). **FAQ, pp. 1, 3.**

2. Capture is intentionally not the endpoint of a to-do list. The process says to get ideas, wants, needs, actions, and communications onto paper, then “chunk” related items around a shared Result. **FAQ, p. 2.**

3. A plan groups related actions into a clear Result, a compelling Purpose, and a flexible MAP/menu of options. The worksheet also records leverage person, duration, priority, total time, and total “must” time; it explicitly says every action need not be completed to obtain the Result. **FAQ, p. 2.**

4. Result and Purpose are distinct: the Result is externally understandable and ideally measurable; the Purpose is internal/emotional and explains why the Result matters. The FAQ gives verbs such as “create” and “resolve” as Result cues and “because,” “in order to,” and “so that” as Purpose cues. **FAQ, p. 3.**

5. Priorities are importance rankings, not necessarily execution order. The FAQ recommends numbering MAP actions by priority; sequential dependencies may mean a high-priority action is completed later. **FAQ, p. 6.**

6. The weekly example turns Results into scheduled work: Diego blocks roughly 1–2 hours each day for important Results and schedules other appointments around that block time. It asks for the top 3–5 weekly Results and likely challenges/game plans. **Weekly Plan, Diego, p. 3.**

7. The source materials describe recurring planning/review rhythms: a weekly planning session (suggested 60–90 minutes on Friday or Sunday), weekday-morning daily planning, and regular review of the annual Life Plan and Project Plans. The FAQ also recommends a weekly five-to-ten-minute practice of celebrating victories; Diego’s example includes an end-of-week accomplishments/progress/impact reflection and carry-over prompts. **FAQ, pp. 2, 6; Weekly Plan, Diego, pp. 3–4.**

8. Categories of Improvement are personal/professional life areas committed to for continuous improvement, with examples including health, relationships, finances, mind/emotions, spirituality, and contribution. Amelia’s category worksheet shows that categories can be personalized and paired with identity/role descriptions (e.g., “Adventure” and “Explorer, Trailblazer, Trendy Traveler”). **FAQ, p. 4; Categories, Amelia, p. 1.**

## Explicit limits / absent information

- These PDFs show calendar-like time blocking on a worksheet, but do **not** specify Google Calendar integration, calendar API behavior, automatic conflict detection, or digital time-zone handling.
- They mention recurring daily/weekly planning and habits, but do **not** define a data model or automation semantics for recurring tasks (for example, skip, reschedule, or completion-instance rules).
- They provide a framework for purposes, roles, and categories, but do **not** specify how to ingest a person’s broader life context or how software should generate recommendations; no efficacy evidence or outcome evaluation is supplied in these PDFs.
